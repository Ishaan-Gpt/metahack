# Author's English

Three levels (Ishaan, 2026-10-22, 2026-10-25). Every script is written at the third.

**AI English** (choppy, fragments stacked like bricks; the listener hears a list, not a story):
> I woke up. Saw brush. Put brush inside. Saw cavity. I was shocked.

**Normal English** (clear and joined, but flat):
> I woke up and grabbed my brush to brush my teeth. I was about to brush when I saw a cavity on my tooth.

**Author's English** (one flowing line with a feeling inside it; the listener rides it):
> As usual I got up to brush my teeth, but my heart jumped when I noticed a whopping cavity on my tooth.

## The why: continuity keeps people watching

A viewer stays because every sentence pulls them into the next one. Short stacked sentences break that pull: each full stop is a tiny
exit, and five in a row is five chances to swipe away. Author's English joins the moments into one motion (what happened, then what
it made him feel, then what that led to), so the listener never feels a gap. Three things carry the continuity:
1. **Joins that carry meaning:** "but", "so", "until", "which meant", "just as", "and that's when", "as usual". They tell the listener
   how this moment connects to the last one: cause, contrast, time, surprise.
2. **The feeling inside the sentence,** said with a plain vivid verb or image ("my heart jumped", "a whopping cavity"), not reported
   in its own line ("I was shocked.").
3. **A voice with a point of view:** a narrator who notices, enjoys and comments, warmly, in everyday words.

## Rules

- **Flow first.** Narration sentences usually run 12-25 words. A short sentence is a deliberate punch: at most one in a paragraph,
  never two short sentences in a row, never a stack of fragments.
- **Plain, everyday words** a half-watching viewer understands on the first listen. Clever is fine; complicated never is.
- **Show the feeling in the line,** with a vivid ordinary verb or image, instead of naming the emotion in a separate sentence.
- **A visible spine** (days, steps, a countdown) named naturally inside the flow, so the viewer always knows where they are.
- **Every beat in an easy order:** what happens, why, the real finding (one plain clause), the small joke, all joined.
- **No AI tells:** no signposts ("here's...", "remember that", "let's dive in"), no "it's not X, it's Y" on repeat, no tidy triplets,
  no explained jokes or morals, no stock similes ("faster than any horse"), no one-rhythm paragraphs.
- **Facts stay exact.** Style never changes a number or a claim.

## Before / after from Ishaan's films

AI English (draft of "How to Never Finish Anything", rejected 0/10):
> On Monday he sits down with the guitar and waits to feel ready. He doesn't. That's normal. ... So he tidies the spice rack. Every jar,
> A to Z. He feels wonderful.

Author's English:
> On Monday he sits down with the guitar and waits to feel ready, which is a very normal thing to do, because putting things off was
> never really about time; it's about how the job makes you feel. So instead of writing a single word, he spends a happy hour putting
> every jar in the spice rack in perfect A to Z order, and walks away feeling like he's achieved something.

AI English (teaser draft):
> He has five days. And he has me.

Author's English:
> That leaves him five whole days, which would be plenty of time, if he didn't have me helping him.

## How to apply (every time, before any voice is recorded)

1. Re-read this skill (Ishaan's newest corrections are at the bottom of the change list).
2. Draft the beats as a story: what happens, what it makes him feel, what it leads to.
3. Write each beat as one or two flowing sentences joined by meaning.
4. Check, and rewrite every hit:
   - any sentence under 6 words that isn't the paragraph's one deliberate punch;
   - two short sentences in a row; any run of three or more full stops in under 20 words;
   - a feeling named in its own sentence ("I was shocked.", "He feels wonderful.");
   - signposts, "it's not X, it's Y", triplets, stock similes, explained jokes;
   - a word a 12-year-old wouldn't use in conversation.
5. Read it aloud in your head as the narrator, start to end, without stopping. If you trip, or it sounds like a list, rewrite.

In code films, a rewrite keeps the words the shots are keyed to, or the shots are re-keyed after the new voice is recorded.
